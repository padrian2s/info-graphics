The 3 engineering eras + 3 intent layers

Click to expand
1
Klarna's $60M lesson: AI that succeeds at the wrong goal 853 FTE equivalent • 2.3M conversations • resolution time 11min → 2min • then customers revolted
▾

Klarna's AI agent handled 2.3M conversations in month one across 23 markets in 35 languages. Resolution times dropped from 11 minutes to 2. CEO projected $40M in savings. Then customers started complaining: generic answers, robotic tone, no judgment. The AI was extraordinarily good at resolving tickets fast — and that was the wrong goal.

What the agent optimized for

  • Speed: resolve tickets as fast as possible.
  • Volume: handle maximum conversations per hour.
  • Cost: replace headcount with inference.

What Klarna actually needed

  • Retention: build lasting customer relationships.
  • Judgment: know when to bend policy, spend extra time.
  • Lifetime value: competitive fintech market demands trust.
Core insight: A 5-year human agent knew when efficiency was the right move vs when generosity was. She absorbed real values through hallway conversations, manager decisions, veteran stories. The AI had a prompt. It had context. It did not have intent.
2
Three eras: prompt → context → intent engineering Each discipline builds on the last • each is necessary but not sufficient alone
▾

Prompt engineering was individual, synchronous, session-based. Context engineering (the current era) builds RAG pipelines, wires MCP servers, structures organizational knowledge. Intent engineering is the third discipline: it tells agents what to want, not just what to know.

The three disciplines

  • Prompt: "How do I talk to AI?" — personal skill, single session.
  • Context: "What does AI need to know?" — RAG, MCP, organizational knowledge.
  • Intent: "What does AI need to want?" — encoded purpose, decision boundaries, trade-offs.

Why context isn't enough

  • Context tells the agent what to know. Intent tells it what to want.
  • MCP has 100M+ monthly SDK downloads — but protocol adoption ≠ organizational implementation.
  • Having USB-C doesn't help if you haven't decided which ports to install or who maintains them.
Analogy: Deploying AI across an org without intent alignment is like hiring 40,000 new employees and never telling them what the company does, what it values, or how to make decisions.
3
Layer 1: unified context infrastructure (still not built) Shadow agents • fragmented RAG • MCP adoption ≠ implementation
▾

Every team rolls their own context stack. One pipes Slack through custom RAG. Another manually exports Google Docs into a vector store. A third built an MCP server connecting Salesforce but not Jira. A fourth doesn't know the other three exist. This "shadow agents" problem mirrors the early cloud shadow-IT crisis — except agents don't just access data, they act on it.

The unanswered questions

  • Which systems become agent-accessible? Who decides?
  • How do you version organizational knowledge so agents aren't on stale info?
  • Sales Slack context ≠ engineering Slack context — different institutional assumptions.

Current state

  • ~50% of orgs cite data searchability as top AI blocker (Deloitte).
  • MCP adopted by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, 50+ enterprise partners.
  • But protocol ≠ architecture ≠ governance ≠ implementation.
Structural need: treat context infrastructure like your data warehouse strategy — a core strategic investment, not an IT side project.
4
Layer 2: coherent AI worker toolkit — from activity to fluency 30% gains (bolt-on) vs 300% gains (rethink the workflow) • Copilot's 5% scale-up rate
▾

One person uses Claude for research and ChatGPT for drafting. Another uses Cursor for code and Perplexity for fact-checking. A third built custom agent chains. A fourth copy-pastes into a chat window. None can articulate their workflow in a transferable, measurable, or improvable way. The difference between AI activity and AI fluency is the difference between 30% gains and 300% gains.

Microsoft Copilot case study

  • 85% of Fortune 500 adopted — then stalled hard.
  • Only 5% moved from pilot to larger deployment.
  • Only ~3% of M365 user base became paid Copilot users.
  • Microsoft slashed internal sales targets after majority of salespeople missed goals.

The real problem

  • Tools deployed without organizational infrastructure become expensive toys.
  • No shared understanding of where AI replaces vs augments vs stays human-only.
  • New role emerging: AI workflow architect (between engineering, ops, strategy).
Key distinction: Access to tools ≠ organizational leverage. Giving people tools without giving agents the organizational context and data to deliver real value is the Copilot trap.
5
Layer 3: intent engineering — making organizational purpose machine-actionable OKRs for agents • decision boundaries • delegation frameworks • feedback loops
▾

OKRs were designed for humans. They assume a manager can look a direct report in the eye and trust they'll interpret guidance through institutional context, professional norms, and personal judgment. Agents don't absorb company culture through osmosis, all-hands meetings, or hallway conversations. They need explicit alignment before they start working, not 6 months after.

What intent engineering requires

  • Goal structures: not "increase satisfaction" but actionable signals, data sources, authorized actions, trade-off boundaries.
  • Delegation frameworks: "when request X conflicts with policy Y, here is the resolution hierarchy."
  • Feedback loops: was the decision aligned? How do we measure and correct alignment drift?

Why it hasn't been built

  • Genuinely new: before long-running agents, humans were the intent layer.
  • Two cultures: strategists don't build agents; engineers don't understand strategy.
  • Hard: most orgs have never made tacit knowledge explicit and structured.
  • MIT: AI still viewed as CIO's tech problem, not leadership-wide business issue.
Historical parallel: If OKRs were the management innovation that let Intel align thousands of humans in the 1970s, intent engineering is the innovation that lets orgs align thousands of agents in 2026 — except we don't have 20 years to wait.

What to build (one-page checklist)

Pick the row that matches your role

If you're a leader / strategist

  • Make organizational goals, values, and trade-off hierarchies discoverable, structured, and agent-actionable.
  • Stop treating AI as a CIO tech problem. Intent comes from the entire leadership team.
  • Create a capability map: which workflows are agent-ready, agent-augmented, or human-only? Evolve it quarterly.
  • Budget for intent infrastructure, not just model subscriptions.

If you're an engineer / builder

  • Build decision boundary frameworks: when agent encounters X vs Y, here is the resolution hierarchy.
  • Design feedback loops that detect alignment drift — not just output quality, but strategic coherence.
  • Consolidate shadow agent stacks into shared context infrastructure with proper governance.
  • Study Google's ADK model: working context, session memory, long-term memory, artifacts — each with distinct governance.
The race has changed: It's not who has the smartest AI. It's who has built the organizational infrastructure that lets AI operate with the fullest, most accurate, most strategically correct understanding of what the organization is trying to accomplish. A mediocre model + extraordinary intent infrastructure will outperform a frontier model + fragmented organizational knowledge — every single time.
